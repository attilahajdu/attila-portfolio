Wait. Why does AI need water?

The AI itself doesn't drink water. The infrastructure running it can.

1

AI runs on powerful computers

When you send a prompt, it usually travels to a large data centre, a building full of racks of specialised chips built to run AI models very fast.

your device → data centre → server rack → AI chip

For large models, most serving energy is drawn by AI accelerators (TPUs or GPUs), with host CPUs, memory, idle provisioning and facility overhead adding roughly 40 to 60 percent on top of active-chip energy in production measurements [1].

2

Computation produces heat

Powerful chips generate a lot of heat while running AI models, thousands of calculations happening at once. That heat has to go somewhere.

chip working → heat rising
3

That heat must be removed

Data centres don't all cool themselves the same way. Air cooling uses little water. Closed liquid loops recirculate. Evaporative cooling deliberately evaporates water to carry heat away, and that water is consumed. Which system served your request is usually not disclosed, and that is one of the biggest reasons our results are ranges rather than exact numbers.

air cooling · closed liquid loop · evaporative cooling (consumes water)

The standard metric is WUE, water usage effectiveness, in litres per kWh of IT energy. Published fleet averages range from roughly 0.3 to 1.6 L/kWh across hyperscale operators, and individual sites vary far more [8]. We model WUE as a distribution, never a single number, and we only ever use consumptive WUE, never withdrawal figures.

4

Electricity can consume water too

Zoom out from the data centre and you reach the electricity grid. Many power stations, especially thermal ones, consume water for their own cooling while generating electricity. So even a data centre that uses little cooling water can still carry an upstream water footprint.

data centre → grid → power generation → water consumption

Consumptive water intensity of generation varies by technology, from near zero for wind and solar PV to several litres per kWh for some thermal and hydro accounting methods [7]. We model a grid-average distribution and treat it as the largest single upstream unknown after model energy.

Direct cooling water+ Electricity-related water= Operational water footprint

We report the operational footprint only. Manufacturing chips and building data centres also use water, but the uncertainty is far larger, so we never silently mix it into the headline number. And the water doesn't vanish: evaporated cooling water rejoins the water cycle, just often somewhere other than where it was drawn.

            About this tool

            What Drip is, and what it stands on

            What this is

            Drip is an independent estimator of the water footprint behind everyday AI use. You describe a task in ordinary language, a chat session, some images, a video, an afternoon of AI-assisted coding, take a guess, and get back a confident floor, a typical range and a best estimate, shown in household terms like toilet flushes, shower minutes and kettle boils.

            Every figure is a modelled estimate traceable to a numbered public source. None of it is a measurement of any company's actual systems, and nothing here is a claim about what any specific provider consumed. Where a provider has published its own measurements we say so and cite them; where it hasn't, we extrapolate from independent research, grade the evidence lower and widen the range accordingly.

            Two audiences, one tool

            Everyday mode speaks plainly ("usually between 8 mL and 24 mL, very unlikely to be under 6 mL") and includes a guess-before-reveal game, a water-literacy quiz, session badges and shareable receipts. Expert mode opens the machinery: percentiles, the estimator path taken, a density plot of all 8,000 simulated scenarios, live sliders for facility and grid parameters, a switchable water-accounting boundary, and the full coefficient table with evidence grades and citations.

            How the estimate is built

            The engine keeps two water pathways separate, because most public commentary conflates them: water consumed cooling the data centre on site, and water consumed upstream generating the electricity. Your description is decomposed into individual events, including versions you discarded, which are charged at full cost because the AI ran regardless.

            Energy per event follows an evidence hierarchy: a provider's own production measurement where one exists, a provider claim where one is stated, and independent open-model benchmarks otherwise. The path taken is recorded with every estimate. There is deliberately no universal "water per prompt" number, because no such constant honestly exists.

            Nothing uncertain is treated as a single value. Each estimate runs 8,000 Monte Carlo scenarios, with infrastructure unknowns sampled once per scenario and shared across events so uncertainty stays correlated instead of quietly averaging away. Video uses a segmented model reflecting how tools actually work in 2026: energy rises steeply within a native clip of roughly 5 to 12 seconds, then scales linearly as clips are stitched, with a deliberately wide 4K multiplier because commercial 4K is usually an upscale pass rather than native generation.

            The research this stands on

            Eleven cited sources, each mapped to specific coefficients in the model. The measurement backbone comes from Google's 2025 production study of Gemini (Elsworth, Huang and colleagues), Luccioni, Jernite and Strubell's inference benchmarks across 88 models at ACM FAccT 2024, the 2025 and 2026 video energy scaling papers from Jegham, Gamazaychikov, Delavande, Pierrard and Luccioni, and the AI Energy Score v2 open-model benchmarks.

            The water framework follows Li, Yang, Islam and Ren's two-boundary approach, with grid water intensities from Macknick and colleagues at NREL. Infrastructure distributions draw on the Uptime Institute survey alongside Google's and Microsoft's environmental reports. Provider-side evidence includes Mistral AI's lifecycle assessment with Carbone 4 and ADEME, and OpenAI's stated per-query figure, which is labelled as a claim rather than a measurement because its methodology is unpublished.

            The 2026 United Nations University report on the environmental cost of AI (Aczel, Chamanara, Matin, Farsi, Marwala and Madani, UNU-INWEH) supplies the lifecycle electricity-water boundary that expert mode can switch to, along with per-country intensities and the characterisation of commercial video as stitched short clips.

            Full citations with links are on the of the home page.

            How we check ourselves

            A 28-check automated evaluation runs against the calculation engine: it confirms the model reproduces each cited source at that source's own accounting boundary, that scaling behaviour matches the published laws, that repeated runs agree to within a couple of percent, and that the stated floor is properly calibrated, exactly one in twenty simulated scenarios falls below it.

            The methodology is versioned, and corrections are public rather than silent. Version 0.3 fixed a genuine error in the video duration model after an outside challenge; that change, and the reasoning behind it, is documented. Divergences from other figures circulating online, including the widely quoted 2023 estimate and the larger UN figures, are explained rather than hidden: most turn out to be different accounting boundaries rather than disagreements about the underlying energy.

            What building this cost

            Fair question to turn on ourselves. This tool was built through a multi-day AI-assisted coding session, so we ran that session through Drip's own engine: roughly ≈33 litres of water, almost certainly more than 2 litres, possibly up to ≈260. About 2.7 minutes of showering, or 6 to 7 toilet flushes. Around a third went to data-centre cooling and the rest to the water behind an estimated 14 kWh of electricity.

            The honest details: it's a grade-D, wide-range estimate, exactly as the tool warns for coding sessions, and a good share of it was iteration, including one full rebuild, a corrected video model and several discarded drafts. Was it worth the water? We think so. But the point of this tool is that we got to decide that knowingly, and now so can you.
